## Why this topic
It takes the best long-run space format, survival on other worlds, as a "pick one" question in the second person. Each option has a concrete, checkable deadly twist: 88 days of dark, minus 180 °C, a night that's 470 °C, a 42-year night. The answer surprises: Mars wins, and you'd see Earth as the evening star. The hook is a single true number ("One of these nights lasts 42 years"), and the last line loops back into it. All four worlds use real-data planet maps.

## Fact sources (each claim has 2+ sources)
- Mercury solar day = 176 Earth days, so the Sun stays down ~88 Earth days: NASA Mercury facts (science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts), ESA "Meet Mercury" (176 days), Planetary Society (176 Earth days). WorldAtlas (Aug 2026) gives Mercury's night as roughly 88 Earth days.
- Mercury at night: −180 °C (−290 °F) with no atmosphere to keep the heat: NASA ("Without an atmosphere to retain that heat at night… -290°F (-180°C)"), ESA (−180 °C night), Planetary Society.
- Dry ice sublimates at −78.5 °C (Britannica, Wikipedia; logged in the 2026-11-24 WYR topic), so −180 °C is about 100 degrees colder (derived).
- Venus: sunrise to sunrise ≈ 117 Earth days (NASA, Planetary Society, Space.com), so night ≈ 58 days (derived; WorldAtlas: "roughly 58 Earth days"). The Sun rises in the west and sets in the east (NASA, Space.com).
- Venus is about the same temperature day and night, because the thick CO₂ air traps heat: NASA ("traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect"), Space.com "How hot is Venus", Universe Today "How hot is Venus". Values: 470 °C (Planetary Society), 471 °C (Space.com), 475 °C/900 °F (NASA). We say "near 470 °C".
- Lead melts at 327.5 °C (in the 2026-11-21 skydive sources: Space.com and NASA "hot enough to melt lead").
- Venus surface pressure ≈ 90× Earth's: Space.com ("over 90 times that of Earth"), NASA and Guinness (Venera 13 measured 89 atm), as cited in the 2026-11-21 skydive research.
- Mars sol ≈ 24 h 39–40 min, about 40 min longer than Earth's day: NASA Mars facts and the NSSDC Mars fact sheet (cited in the 2026-11-20 Moon/Mars topic).
- Mars nights around −70 °C: JPL PIA16913 (Curiosity at Gale Crater, daily lows about −70 °C / −94 °F) and Space.com ("at night… about minus 100 degrees F (minus 73 degrees C)").
- Phobos rises in the west and sets in the east: JPL "NASA Orbiter Eyes Phobos over Mars Horizon" (2003: "usually rises in the west and moves rapidly across the sky to set in the east twice a day") and EarthSky (2013: "Because it out-races Mars' rotation, Phobos rises in the west and sets in the east").
- Earth as the evening star from Mars: JPL "NASA Mars Rover Curiosity Sees 'Evening Star' Earth" (Feb 6 2014: Earth "brighter than any star in the Martian night sky", sol 529, Jan 31 2014, about 80 min after sunset) and NASA Earth Observatory "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Earth" (Mar 2014).
- Uranus has no solid surface: NASA Uranus facts ("doesn't have a true surface") and the Smithsonian NASM Uranus page.
- Uranus is tipped on its side (97.77–98°): NASA, EarthSky, Smithsonian NASM, timeanddate.
- Near its poles a night lasts about 42 years: Universe Today ("another 42 years of darkness before the Sun appeared again") and StarDate (Nov 19 2025: "the polar regions have 42 years of daylight followed by 42 years of darkness"). NASA's own page describes a "21-year-long, dark winter" for the hemisphere (the time the Sun is nearly overhead the other pole), and WorldAtlas says 21 years. The 42 years applies at the pole itself (half of the 84-year orbit), so the script says "near its poles… about 42 years".
- 20 + 42 = 62 (arithmetic).

## Visual references
- Uranus: real-data map (kit.planet, Solar System Scope). The pale cyan featureless disc matches Voyager 2's natural-color view. 97.8° tilt with the Sun along the axis (solstice): one hemisphere fully dark.
- Mercury: real MESSENGER-based map. The surface is grey cratered regolith under a black, airless sky with no glow around the setting Sun (Apollo/MESSENGER airless-body lighting).
- Venus: featureless pale-yellow cloud globe (Mariner 10/MESSENGER visible light). The surface is dark basalt slabs as in the Venera 13 panoramas, with a dim, murky lower atmosphere and only the suit lamp at night.
- Mars: real-data map. After sunset the sky is blue near the set Sun (NASA Curiosity/InSight sunset images, as in our Moon/Mars Short), with rust regolith and Earth as a bright point in the west (JPL PIA17936). Phobos is drawn as a small grey, lumpy moon moving west → east.
