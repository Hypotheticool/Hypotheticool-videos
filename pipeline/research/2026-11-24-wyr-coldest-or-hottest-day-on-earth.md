## Fact sources (each claim ≥2)
- Vostok Station, −89.2 °C (−128.6 °F), 21 July 1983: WMO Archive of Weather and Climate Extremes (wmo.int/asu-map?map=Temp_020), Guinness World Records, Wikipedia (Vostok Station).
- Dry ice sublimes at −78.5 °C (−109.3 °F); Vostok record is ~19 °F colder: Wikipedia (Dry ice), Britannica.
- Polar night in July at Vostok (78.5°S: Sun's noon altitude ≈ 90 − 78.5 − 20.4 ≈ −9°): Wikipedia (Vostok Station), solar geometry check; Scientific American "The Coldest Place on Earth".
- Exposed skin can freeze in minutes in extreme cold (NWS wind-chill chart: frostbite in 5 min at wind chills near −50 °F and below; Vostok is far colder): weather.gov wind-chill chart, Mayo Clinic frostbite page. Hedged as "in minutes".
- Satellites found hollows on the East Antarctic ice sheet near −98 °C (−144 °F): AGU press release (Scambos et al. 2018, GRL), National Geographic, Live Science. Not at Vostok itself: on the high ridge between Dome A and Dome Fuji, so the script says "on the ice sheet", not "at Vostok".
- Death Valley (Furnace Creek), 56.7 °C (134 °F), 10 July 1913, still the official WMO world record: WMO archive (Temp_010), NPS Death Valley weather page, Guinness. (Some climatologists dispute it; the script says "officially recorded".)
- Hot tub limit 104 °F: US CPSC hot-tub safety, CDC healthy swimming. 134 − 104 = 30.
- Ground temperature 201 °F (93.9 °C) at Furnace Creek, 15 July 1972: NPS Death Valley weather page, Wikipedia (Death Valley).
- Egg yolk sets at ~149–158 °F (on-screen label only): Exploratorium egg science, USDA FSIS egg handling.
- NPS: drink at least one gallon (4 L) of water a day; heat deaths of visitors: NPS Death Valley safety page, NPS heat press kit.
- Summer crowds visit Death Valley: NPS heat press kit (summer visitors from around the world), Wikipedia (Death Valley National Park).
- 300 Club at the South Pole: 200 °F sauna, then outside when it is −100 °F or colder, around the ceremonial pole: Wikipedia (300 Club), Alaska Public Media (2014), Mental Floss.
