## Fact sources (each claim has 2+ sources)
- Being under a tree is the second leading cause of lightning casualties: NWS lightning myths ("Being underneath a tree is the second leading cause of lightning casualties"); Environment Canada (20% of lightning deaths are people sheltering under a tree or open gazebo); CDC FAQ ("Do not take shelter under a tree").
- Side flash jumps from a struck tree to someone a foot or two away: NWS "How lightning strikes people" ("Most often, side flash victims have taken shelter under a tree"); Environment Canada "Dangers of lightning".
- Water: avoid all water during a thunderstorm, get out of lakes and ponds. Ground current spreads along the surface, especially through wet ground or water: CDC FAQ, NOAA JetStream lightning safety, UGA Extension climate blog (2017).
- Ground current causes the most lightning deaths and injuries, and direct strikes only 3–5%: NWS "How lightning strikes people" ("the ground current causes the most lightning deaths and injuries"); Environment Canada (ground current 40–50%, side flash 20–30%, direct strike 3–5% of injuries); UGA Extension ("direct strikes only cause issues 3-5 percent of the time").
- Lying flat increases ground-current exposure: NWS myths ("Lying flat increases your chance of being affected by potentially deadly ground current"); NOAA JetStream ("never lie flat on the ground"); CDC ("Do NOT lie on the ground").
- A hard-topped metal vehicle is safe because the metal shell carries the current around you, not the rubber tires. Keep the windows up and your hands in your lap. Convertibles are not safe: NWS myths ("Run to a substantial building or hard topped vehicle"); NOAA JetStream ("Rubber soles of shoes (or rubber tires) … offer zero lightning protection"; "Convertible vehicles offer no safety from lightning"); Environment Canada ("the metal shell of a car provides a pathway for the lightning strike to flow around the vehicle… hardtop metal roof (not a convertible)"; "hands on your lap"); CDC (convertibles, golf carts and motorcycles are not safe).
- Lightning heats the air to 50,000 °F, five times hotter than the Sun's surface: NWS "How hot is lightning?"; National Geographic "What is lightning?". The Sun's surface is about 10,000 °F (5,500 °C) per NASA Sun facts.
- Roy Sullivan, a Shenandoah National Park ranger in Virginia, was struck 7 times between 1942 and 1977 and survived every strike (Guinness record): Guinness World Records "Most lightning strikes survived"; Wikipedia "Roy Sullivan"; Mental Floss.
- About 10% of people struck by lightning die, so about 9 in 10 survive: CDC FAQ ("About 10% of people struck by lightning die"); NWS "How lightning strikes people".
- Not used: Sullivan's later death (irrelevant to the topic and sensitive) and the US deaths per year (CDC 26/yr for 2006–2025 vs NOAA's "20–30"; it isn't needed).
