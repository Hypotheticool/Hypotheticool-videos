## Fact sheet (each claim, 2+ sources)
- Equator surface speed ~1,000 mph / ~1,670 km/h (1,037 mph): Space.com "What would happen if Earth stopped spinning?" (2024, A. Layden, BGSU); Astronomy.com; BBC Sky at Night (P. Wozniakiewicz, Kent: "over 400 m/s"); Universe Today (1,674 km/h); Esri ArcUser (1,667 km/h).
- Faster than sound (~761 mph at sea level, 15 °C; ~767 mph at 20 °C): NASA Glenn Beginner's Guide "Speed of Sound" / "Mach number" (grc.nasa.gov); Live Science "What Is the Speed of Sound?"; Aerospaceweb standard atmosphere. 1,037 > 761.
- New York ~790 mph: computed 1,037 mph × cos(40.7°) = 786 mph (rotation speed scales with cos latitude; Sky at Night / Universe Today: poles barely move).
- Everything not attached keeps moving east at up to that speed: Space.com; Sky at Night ("we'd all get flung eastwards"); Universe Today ("more than 1,600 km/hour sideways"); Astronomy ("people, houses, and trees… hundreds of miles an hour").
- The air keeps moving and scours the surface: Astronomy ("high speed winds, still rotating nearly as fast as the planet, would scour the surface clean"); LADbible on the Zack D. Films simulation ("powerful winds would blast across the planet"). Script hedges to "near the equator".
- Strongest tornado winds measured ~300–320 mph: NWS Norman (Bridge Creek–Moore, May 3 1999, DOW 301 ± 20 mph); Greenfield, Iowa tornado (May 21 2024, DOW-measured ~309–318 mph): NBC News "Storm chasers catch tornado with 300 mph winds", Star Tribune "Winds reached 300 mph in Greenfield, Iowa, tornado", Weatherzone/DTN "some of the strongest winds ever observed on Earth". On screen: "~300+ MPH". 1,000 ÷ 318 ≈ 3.1 → "over three times".
- Oceans slosh east, giant tsunamis: Space.com (tsunamis likely); Universe Today (oceans slosh sideways at 1,600 km/h); LADbible/Zack D. Films ("giant tsunami").
- Near the poles you'd barely feel it: Space.com ("less affected… at or near the poles"); Universe Today ("you'd barely even feel it").
- One day would last a year; ~6 months of light then ~6 months of dark: Universe Today ("365 days for the Sun to move through the sky… 6 months of night, followed by 6 months of day"); Esri ("a day would last as long as a year"); Space.com (days and nights "until each lasted six months").
- Day side very hot, night side very cold: Universe Today; Sky at Night (permanent-night side "becoming extremely cold"). No numbers used.
- Magnetic field might weaken (hedged "might"): Space.com; Astronomy ("would likely mean the end of our magnetic field"); Sky at Night ("possible loss").
- Ocean bulge ~8 km (~5 miles) at the equator, held out by the spin: Esri ArcUser (Fraczek: "about 8 km high at the equator"); Universe Today ("by 8 km"). Comparison: Everest 8,849 m → 8 km ≈ 90% → "almost as tall as Everest".
- Without spin the water flows to the poles, leaving one equatorial megacontinent: Esri ArcUser ("a huge equatorial megacontinent and two large polar oceans"); Universe Today ("a single continent around the middle of the planet"); Popular Science / Gizmodo (2010) on the Esri model. On screen: SIMPLIFIED (ESRI MODEL).
- Earth is slowing: tides add ~1.7–2 ms per day per century: Scientific American ("1.7 milliseconds… each century"); Astronomy (1.7 ms); Discover ("2 milliseconds every 100 years"); CORDIS. Script: "almost two thousandths of a second".
- 1.4 billion years ago a day was ~18 hours: Discover (PNAS, Meyers & Malinverno, 2018); UW–Madison news release on the same study.
- Earth won't fully stop: Space.com ("very unlikely that Earth will ever stop rotating"); Astronomy ("should never stop spinning completely").
