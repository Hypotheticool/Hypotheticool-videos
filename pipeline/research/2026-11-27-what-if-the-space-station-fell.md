## Fact sheet (each claim, 2+ sources; titles seen in search results, plus NASA figures)
- Length ~357 ft, "one yard shy of a football field" (360 ft incl. end zones); mass 925,335 lb; NASA's comparison: "more than 320 automobiles"; ~17,500 mph, 16 orbits a day, ~250 miles up: NASA Space Station Facts and Figures (nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/); Space.com "International Space Station: By the Numbers"; CBS News ISS statistics; NASA ISS diagram fact sheet PDF (discoverspace.org).
- Coast to coast in under 10 minutes: NY–LA great circle ≈ 2,450 mi ÷ 17,500 mph ≈ 8.4 min (computed; the on-screen counter shows this).
- Thin air drags on the station so it slowly sinks and needs regular reboosts: issinfo.net "Why its orbit, altitude and speed constantly change"; NASA ISS transition FAQ; Planetary Society "How NASA plans to deorbit the ISS".
- Docked crew vehicles serve as lifeboats: NASA ISS FAQ; Planetary Society deorbit article.
- Reentry: the air ahead of the craft is compressed and shock-heated until it glows (thousands of degrees, hotter than lava's ~700–1,200 °C): NASA Orbital Debris Program Office "Debris Reentry" (orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/reentry/); Aerospace Corporation reentry FAQ (aerospace.org). Script says "hotter than lava" without a number.
- Main breakup ~78 km (~48 mi, "about fifty miles up"); solar arrays and appendages come off first: Aerospace Corporation reentry FAQ; NASA ODPO reentry page; ESA Space Debris Conference papers (SDC4 paper 44).
- "Ten to forty percent" of a large object's mass can survive to the ground: Aerospace Corporation ("typically 10–40%"); NASA ODPO reentry page / NASA ISS Deorbit Analysis Summary (nasa.gov 2024 PDF: an uncontrolled reentry of the ISS would put large debris at risk of hitting populated areas). "Tens of tons" = 10% of ~420 t ≈ 42 t (hedged with "could").
- Orbit inclination 51.6°, so the ground track spans 51.6 N to 51.6 S, where most people live: NASA EOL "Space Station Orbit Tutorial"; NASA Earth Observatory "An Orbital Tour"; NASA SVS "Comparing ISS and ICESat-2 Coverage". Script: "crosses over most of the people on Earth".
- Skylab, ~77 t, fell July 1979 (July 11 UTC), debris across Western Australia, nobody hurt: Space.com "40 Years Ago, NASA's Skylab Space Station Fell to Earth"; 19FortyFive (June 2026, "77-ton Skylab"); watchers.news; NBC News "Got $4? Go see Skylab's remains".
- Esperance fined NASA $400 for littering; paid in 2009 (30 years later) by a US radio host (Scott Barley, Highway Radio): Guinness World Records "First fine for littering from space"; Mental Floss "NASA's unpaid $400 littering ticket"; PlanSponsor "Was Skylab's littering fine paid?"; Space Connect "Remembering NASA's $400 fine".
- NASA hired SpaceX to build the US Deorbit Vehicle to steer the ISS down around 2030 into a remote part of the South Pacific: NASA news release "NASA Selects International Space Station US Deorbit Vehicle"; TechCrunch (Jun 26 2024, $843M); Space.com; Aviation Week.
- Point Nemo, the oceanic pole of inaccessibility (48.9 S, 123.4 W), ~2,688 km / 1,670 mi from the nearest land; the closest humans are often astronauts on the ISS; spacecraft graveyard where the ISS will end: BBC Sky at Night "Point Nemo"; Discover Magazine "Point Nemo … Will Be the Final Resting Place of the ISS"; Interesting Engineering; Geographical.
