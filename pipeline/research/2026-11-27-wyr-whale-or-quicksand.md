## Facts and sources (each line has 2+)
- Humpbacks: adults ~49–52 ft (some up to 60 ft) > a ~40 ft school bus: NOAA Fisheries species page; National Geographic; Mass.gov.
- Lunge feeding, >5,000 gallons (23,000 L) per mouthful: Live Science (2025); NPR 2021 (Kerr: "10 SUVs worth of water and fish"); Smithsonian (wide billowing mouths as a net).
- Packard: June 11, 2021, off Provincetown (Cape Cod), ~45 ft deep; "this huge bump and everything went dark"; "Oh my God, I'm in a whale's mouth" (WBZ via NPR); ~30–40 s in the mouth; spat out at the surface; "very bruised up but have no broken bones" (NPR; Smithsonian adds a dislocated knee, so the script says "badly bruised, but no broken bones").
- Throat "roughly the size of a human fist", stretches to ~15 in: National Geographic; NPR 2025.
- Spat out because it can't swallow a person / didn't want him: NPR (Kerr, Robbins); National Geographic (Hodgins).
- Simancas, Strait of Magellan, Chile, Feb 8, 2025; engulfed for a few seconds, surfaced; "I thought I was dead": NPR 2025; Live Science 2025; CBS News.
- Quicksand = sand so saturated with water that friction between grains drops; it liquefies under a small stress change: Nature news (2005); Wikipedia; National Geographic.
- Density ~2 g/mL vs body ~1 g/mL, so you float about waist-deep: National Geographic; Live Science; BBC Science Focus; Wikipedia.
- Pulling a foot out (at ~1 cm/s) takes the force needed to lift a (medium-sized) car: Nature news (Bonn et al., Nature 437, 635, 2005); National Geographic. Attributed on screen as "Nature study, 2005".
- Real danger = rising tide (also cold, thirst): Nature (Bonn); BBC Science Focus; Wikipedia.
- Escape: stay calm, lean/lie back, spread out, wiggle legs slowly so water seeps in: Live Science (Bonn); National Geographic (Bonn); BBC Science Focus.
- Verdict: in both documented humpback cases the person was out in under a minute (Packard ~30–40 s; Simancas a few seconds); experts say a humpback physically cannot swallow a person (NatGeo, NPR). Quicksand rarely kills by itself but can hold you until the tide arrives. Both are rare and survivable; the whale ends faster.
- On-screen comparisons: 45 ft ≈ a 4-story building (~10–12 ft per story); throat ≈ fist (NatGeo/NPR wording); shoulders (~18 in) wider than the ~15 in stretched throat.
