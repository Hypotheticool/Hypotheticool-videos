## Fact sheet (each claim ≥2 sources)
- Cold shock: the first ~1 minute, get your breathing under control (CTV quoting Giesbrecht; McGill OSS; CSBC "Cold Shock will pass in about 1 minute").
- 1-10-1: 1 minute to control breathing, ~10 minutes of meaningful movement / usable minutes to get out, ~1 hour before unconsciousness from hypothermia (McGill OSS: Giesbrecht developed it; CTV; CSBC; AccuWeather). Caveat: NCCWS (coldwatersafety.org) argues the rule understates risk; the script says "about an hour" and doesn't promise safety.
- Self-rescue: turn back toward the direction you came (the last place that held you), hands/arms on the unbroken ice, kick your feet, slide out on your stomach, don't stand up, roll away (North Dakota Game & Fish; AccuWeather survival experts).
- Ice thickness: under 4 in of new clear ice, stay off; 4 in for a person on foot (Minnesota DNR thickness guideline; North Dakota Game & Fish; AccuWeather). On screen: 4 in = 4 stacked hockey pucks (puck 1 in thick: USA Hockey rule 306; Wikipedia).
- Anna Bågenholm, 29, skiing near Narvik, Norway, 20 May 1999: fell head-first through the ice of a frozen stream (Wikipedia; CBS News 2000; ScienceAlert).
- Air pocket, conscious ~40 minutes, then circulatory arrest (Wikipedia; CBS).
- 80 minutes in the water before rescue (Wikipedia; CBS; ExplorersWeb).
- Body temperature 13.7 °C (56.7 °F) (Wikipedia; ScienceAlert; ExplorersWeb; Lancet 2000 paper title, PubMed 10665559). On screen: "one of the lowest ever survived" (Wikipedia's wording; ScienceAlert says "lowest ever recorded"; the hedge covers both).
- 100+ doctors and nurses, nine hours; heart-lung (bypass) machine warmed her blood; Tromsø University Hospital (Wikipedia; CBS; ScienceAlert; ExplorersWeb).
- Later a radiologist at the same hospital (Wikipedia; ExplorersWeb; ScienceAlert).
- Cut as unverifiable or conflicting: exact time her heart restarted (Wikipedia 22:15 same night vs ScienceAlert "next day"), the previous record (14.4 °C vs 12.7 °C claims), wake-up day (10 vs 12 days).
